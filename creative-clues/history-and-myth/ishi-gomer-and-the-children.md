# Ishi, Gomer, and the Children

**Case question:** What does the “Ishi” clue refer to, and does its
associated mother-and-children story reveal a maternal line to Mary?

## The name clue

**Source clue.** In [Hosea 2:16–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%202%3A16-20&version=NRSVUE),
the divine speaker says the addressee will call him **Ishi**, “my
husband,” rather than **Baali**, “my Baal” or “my master.” The next verse
speaks of removing the names of the Baals. Some Hebrew editions number
this passage **Hosea 2:18–22**, two verses later than this English
translation.

The chapter uses marriage imagery for the relationship between God and
Israel. It is not a named divine couple's biological family tree. The
word *baal* can also mean “master” or “husband”; its use here and the
reference to Baal worship should be read in context rather than treated
as proof that two gods are identical or that a historical family line
was replaced.

**Mythic possibility.** A character finds two competing names in an old
record and asks whether the change signals a new relationship, a change
of authority, or a later editor's preference. Each explanation is a
different story possibility, not a conclusion dictated by the word.

**Case question.** Who is speaking, who is addressed, and is the passage
describing people, a community through metaphor, or a divine genealogy?

## Gomer and the named children

**Source clue.** Earlier, [Hosea 1:2–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%201%3A2-9&version=NRSVUE)
names **Gomer, daughter of Diblaim**, and describes three children she
bears. Their names are part of the prophetic message:

| Child of Gomer in the narrative | Named meaning in the translation's notes | What follows in the text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Jezreel**, son | “God sows” | The first son is explicitly said to be born to Hosea. |
| **Lo-ruhamah**, daughter | “Not pitied” | The narrative says Gomer bears her; it does not explicitly identify her father. |
| **Lo-ammi**, son | “Not my people” | Gomer bears him after weaning Lo-ruhamah; the text does not explicitly identify his father. |

The absence of an explicit paternal statement for the latter children
does not prove a different father. [Hosea 2:1, 21–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hosea%202%3A1%3B2%3A21-23&version=NRSVUE)
reuses the names and their meanings in language of renewed belonging and
mercy. The literary meaning of a name is not a DNA marker.

**Maternal-thread test.** On the story's own terms, all three are born
to Gomer; Lo-ruhamah is the named daughter through whom a maternal
branch could continue if she had children. These passages do not name
her children or Gomer's mother, and they do not establish a maternal
connection to Mary or Jesus. Scholars differ on how literally to read
Hosea's marriage narrative; see [Tamara Cohn Eskenazi's discussion](https://www.thetorah.com/article/hoseas-abusive-marital-metaphor-ends-with-courtship-not-violence).

**Mythic possibility.** A missing daughter could recover a family name
that a public record used as a symbol of rejection, then reinterpret it
as a promise of belonging. Her descendants would be invented unless a
source identifies them.

**Case question.** Do other traditions give Gomer or Lo-ruhamah a mother,
children, or a connection to another family? Date each witness and keep
later inventions separate from Hosea's own account.

## Read the whole metaphor

Hosea 2 includes threats and humiliating imagery as well as a later
promise of tenderness and renewal. Interpretations differ; the
[scholarly discussion](https://www.thetorah.com/article/hoseas-abusive-marital-metaphor-ends-with-courtship-not-violence)
considers both. A creative retelling can choose a relationship based on
consent and mutual respect without presenting that choice as a direct
description of every part of the biblical chapter.
