# Passover, Hyssop, and the Grail

The chat asks about blood on a branch, a possible missing cup or dessert at
the Last Supper, and a vessel catching blood. These are related images, but
they come from different texts and periods.

## The branch clue

In [Exodus 12:5–13, 22–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2012%3A5-13%2C22-23&version=NRSVUE),
the Israelites use hyssop to apply lamb's blood to their doorposts and lintel
as a sign in the Passover story. In
[John 19:28–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2019%3A28-30&version=NRSVUE),
hyssop holds a sponge of sour wine to Jesus's mouth before he dies. The
shared plant and Passover setting make a concrete literary comparison; John
does not say that the branch at the cross was dipped in the Passover lamb's
blood or that it was the same physical branch.

## The supper and the four-cup theory

- [Mark 14:12, 22–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014%3A12%2C22-26&version=NRSVUE)
  presents a Passover meal, bread and a cup, Jesus's statement about not
  drinking the fruit of the vine again until the kingdom, and a hymn before
  departure.
- [Luke 22:14–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A14-20&version=NRSVUE)
  places a cup before the bread and also describes a cup after supper in
  this translation; some ancient manuscripts have a shorter form of the
  passage. [1 Corinthians 11:23–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2011%3A23-26&version=NRSVUE)
  preserves a separate early account of bread and a cup after supper.
- [John 18:28 and 19:14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2018%3A28%3BJohn%2019%3A14&version=NRSVUE)
  place references to eating Passover and its preparation after the preceding
  meal, creating a chronological tension with Mark's presentation. Readers
  and scholars propose different explanations.

The later rabbinic [Mishnah Pesachim, chapter 10](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Pesachim.10.1?lang=bi)
describes four cups. It was compiled around 200 CE, though it may preserve
earlier practices; the exact relationship to first-century meals is debated.
For research on that development, see Joshua Kulp,
[“The Origins of the Seder and Haggadah”](https://doi.org/10.1177/1476993X05055642).

The chat presented a missing fourth cup and an omitted afikoman as established
historical facts. The Gospel accounts do not number the cup as the third,
describe a fourth omitted cup, or name an afikoman. A theological reading
can connect the wine on the cross with an unfinished meal, but it should be
labeled an interpretation rather than a reported Gospel detail. Modern
customs around hiding or retrieving matzah should not automatically be
assumed at the first-century meal.

## The cup and blood

[John 19:34](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2019%3A34&version=NRSVUE)
says blood and water flowed from Jesus's side. It does not say a cup caught
them or that the supper vessel was present. In later medieval Grail
literature, Robert de Boron's *Joseph d'Arimathie* connects Joseph, the
Last Supper vessel, and the crucifixion; see
[Britannica's overview](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Joseph-dArimathie-ou-le-Roman-de-lestoire-dou-Graal).
Later Glastonbury traditions are another layer, not a first-century report.
The Grail tradition is a meaningful literary clue, but it does not establish
a historical bloodline or a verified surviving relic.
