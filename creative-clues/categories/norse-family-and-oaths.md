# Norse Family and Oaths: Sources and Overlays

**Question:** What do the Norse stories actually say about Odin, Loki, Frigg,
Freyja, and Baldr, and which details were added by the shared conversation?

## Odin and Loki's oath

In [*Lokasenna* stanzas 9–10](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna6-10.htm),
Loki reminds Odin that they mixed blood and that Odin promised not to drink
unless both were served. Odin asks Vithar to make room for Loki. The poem
does not narrate when or how they made the pact, explain its full legal
effects, or say it transferred a hereditary lineage. Applying it to John the
Baptist and Jesus is an imaginative comparison, not a source connection.

## Loki's family and binding

In Snorri Sturluson's thirteenth-century
[*Gylfaginning*, chapters 34, 50, and 51](https://mythopedia.com/library/prose-edda-brodeur-1916/gylfaginning/),
Loki and Angrboda are parents of Fenrir, the Midgard serpent, and Hel. The
binding account also names Loki's sons Vali and Nari/Narfi: Vali is turned
into a wolf and kills his brother, whose entrails are used to bind Loki.
Sigyn catches the serpent's venom in a bowl; earthquakes accompany the
venom dripping on Loki. At Ragnarok, bonds break and Loki is freed.

The chat said the binding used **Vali's** entrails; in this version it is
Nari/Narfi's. Another Vali is named as Odin's son in other stories, so do not
assume all people with the same name are one figure. Compare texts and
translations before joining family trees.

## Frigg, Freyja, and Baldr

In the surviving stories, Frigg and Freyja are presented as distinct figures.
The idea that they share an earlier origin is debated, not an established
fact. The chat treated that hypothesis as settled and identified Freyja's
husband Odr with Odin as if proven; similarities between names and stories
are questions for scholarship, not proof of identity.

In [*Gylfaginning* chapters 49–50](https://mythopedia.com/library/prose-edda-brodeur-1916/gylfaginning/),
Frigg seeks protections for Baldr; Loki exploits mistletoe and guides Hodr's
throw. The same narrative tells of Baldr's funeral and Hermod's visit to Hel.
These are mythic accounts, not records of events in Jesus's family.
