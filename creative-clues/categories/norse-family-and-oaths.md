# Norse Family and Oaths: Sources and Overlays

**Question:** What do the Norse stories actually say about Odin, Loki, Frigg,
Freyja, and Baldr, and which details were added by the shared conversation?

## Odin and Loki's oath

In [*Lokasenna* stanzas 9–10](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna6-10.htm),
Loki reminds Odin that they mixed blood and that Odin promised not to drink
unless both were served. Odin asks Vithar to make room for Loki. The poem
does not narrate when or how they made the pact, explain its full legal
effects, or say it transferred a hereditary lineage. Applying it to John the
Baptist and Jesus is an imaginative comparison, not a source connection.

## Loki's family and binding

In Snorri Sturluson's thirteenth-century
[*Gylfaginning*, chapters 34, 50, and 51](https://mythopedia.com/library/prose-edda-brodeur-1916/gylfaginning/),
Loki and Angrboda are parents of Fenrir, the Midgard serpent, and Hel. The
binding account also names Loki's sons Vali and Nari/Narfi: Vali is turned
into a wolf and kills his brother, whose entrails are used to bind Loki.
Sigyn catches the serpent's venom in a bowl; earthquakes accompany the
venom dripping on Loki. At Ragnarok, bonds break and Loki is freed.

The prose ending of [*Lokasenna*](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna61-65.htm)
instead says **Vali's** entrails bind Loki and Narfi becomes a wolf. The
chat's version therefore agrees with that ending but not with Snorri's
account. Another Vali is named as Odin's son in other stories, so do not
assume all people with the same name are one figure.

## Loki and Thor: two episodes

In [*Þrymskviða* stanzas 13–20](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-poetic-edda/thrymskvitha),
**Heimdall** proposes disguising Thor as a bride to recover the hammer.
Loki encourages Thor to accept and accompanies him as a maidservant; he
does not originate the proposal in this poem.

In [*Lokasenna* stanzas 56–64](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna56-60.htm),
Loki does taunt Thor, including over his encounter with Skrymir, before
[leaving](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna61-65.htm). This contradicts
the chat's claim that Loki refrains from insulting Thor.

## Frigg, Freyja, and Baldr

In the surviving stories, Frigg and Freyja are presented as distinct figures.
The idea that they share an earlier origin is debated, not an established
fact. The chat treated that hypothesis as settled and identified Freyja's
husband Odr with Odin as if proven; similarities between names and stories
are questions for scholarship, not proof of identity.

For the question about Freyja's children, Snorri's
[*Ynglinga saga*, chapter 13](https://sacred-texts.com/book/heimskringla-or-the-chronicle-of-the-kings-of-norway/the-ynglinga-saga)
names her husband Óðr and her daughters **Hnoss and Gersemi**. This passage
names the daughters but does not explicitly identify their father. Its
mythic family is separate from the proposed maternal ancestry of Jesus.

In [*Gylfaginning* chapters 49–50](https://mythopedia.com/library/prose-edda-brodeur-1916/gylfaginning/),
Frigg seeks protections for Baldr; Loki exploits mistletoe and guides Hodr's
throw. The same narrative tells of Baldr's funeral and Hermod's visit to Hel.
These are mythic accounts, not records of events in Jesus's family.

### Freyja and Loki: accusations versus evidence

In [*Lokasenna* 28–32](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna26-30.htm),
Loki boasts about Baldr's fate, Freyja rebukes him, and he makes sexual
accusations against her. [Freyja calls his tongue false](https://www.voluspa.org/lokasenna31-35.htm).
The poem presents hostile speech, not independent proof of the accusations;
it does not state that romantic jealousy motivated him.

In [*Þrymskviða* 3–4](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-poetic-edda/thrymskvitha),
Freyja lends her feather garment for the search for Thor's hammer. This
shows cooperation in that episode, but does not settle her private feelings
or define every story's relationship.

[*Hyndluljóð* 6–10](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-poetic-edda/hyndluljoth)
has Hyndla accuse Freyja of bringing her lover Óttar; Freyja denies it and
identifies her mount as the boar Hildisvíni. The translator's commentary
reads the mount as Óttar in disguise, but the characters' statements
conflict; the poem should not be reduced to an undisputed romance.
Hyndla then names Óttar's mother **Hledis** and maternal grandmother
**Friaut** in stanza 13. This is a separate mythic genealogy, not a branch
of Mary's documented maternal family.

In the later [*Sörla þáttr*, chapters 1–2](https://www.germanicmythology.com/FORNALDARSAGAS/SorliThattrHedinHogniMorrisTunstall.html),
Loki steals Freyja's necklace on Odin's orders, entering as a fly and
biting her as a flea. Odin demands a destructive conflict as a condition
of its return. The chat combines this with a Heimdall seal-fight account;
that other episode needs its own source rather than being attributed to
these chapters.
