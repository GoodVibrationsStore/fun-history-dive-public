# Teachings and Authority: Before and After Jesus

**Question:** How did religious expectations and arguments about authority
change across different communities and periods?

## Before Jesus: more than one expectation

The Hebrew Bible contains varied texts later interpreted in relation to
messiahship, including [2 Samuel 7:12–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Samuel%207%3A12-16&version=NRSVUE).
Read each in its own setting before comparing later interpretations.

The Dead Sea Scrolls also preserve diverse expectations. The Community Rule,
1QS 9:11, mentions messiahs of Aaron and Israel; this is evidence for an
expectation in that text, not for one uniform belief among all ancient Jews.
See [Livius, Qumran's dual messianism](https://www.livius.org/articles/religion/messiah/messiah-9-two-messiahs/).
Difference among Jewish traditions before Jesus is not evidence that one was
stolen or corrupted.

## Early disagreements after Jesus

- [Galatians 2:11–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Galatians%202%3A11-14&version=NRSVUE):
  Paul recounts opposing Cephas over conduct involving Gentile believers.
  This is Paul's account of a disagreement, not a neutral transcript.
- [Gospel of Mary](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/gospel-of-mary/):
  in the surviving narrative Andrew and Peter challenge Mary's account of a
  vision, while Levi defends her. The surviving Coptic manuscript dates to
  the fourth or fifth century; earlier Greek fragments date to the third.
  Composition is earlier than the manuscripts but its exact date is debated.
  Much of the text is missing.

The Gospel of Mary gives evidence about an early Christian text and its view
of authority. Its dramatic scene alone does not establish that the historical
Peter suppressed the historical Mary, or that its teachings were Jesus's
original words. Compare the text with other early sources before inferring
what happened.

## John the Baptist and Mandaean tradition

The conversation proposes that John's teachings were the original line and
were taken over. Mandaeism is a living religion in which John the Baptist is
an important figure, not simply a Christian "heresy." Different religious
traditions can portray John and Jesus differently, but the existence of those
differences does not prove a takeover. The relationship between Mandaean
traditions and John's historical followers remains a research question.
Consult a scholarly edition of the *Mandaean Book of John* and examine its
dating and manuscript history before comparing individual passages.

## Sophia, Barbelo, and the creator

In the *Apocryphon of John*, Barbelo is the first thought of the transcendent
spirit; Sophia (Wisdom) produces Ialdabaoth, a lesser creator. See Michael
Williams's [discussion of the text](https://www.britannica.com/topic/gnosticism/Apocryphon-of-John).
Three copies are in the Nag Hammadi collection and another is in the Berlin
Codex. This is one ancient mythic interpretation of creation, not a single
doctrine shared by every group now called Gnostic or Hermetic.

The work presents itself as a revelation to John. That literary claim is not
independent proof of its apostolic authorship, an original teaching of Jesus,
or the historical overthrow of a divine ruler. The chat's Rebis-versus-archons
scenario combines traditions; it should be read as an imaginative synthesis
unless a source for the specific connection is identified.

### A closer look at the chat's family tree

In [Frederik Wisse's translation](https://earlychristianwritings.com/text/apocryphonjohn.html),
Barbelo emerges as the Spirit's first thought. The Spirit and Barbelo are
connected with the emergence of the divine Autogenes, and the account lists
Wisdom among three aeons associated with the fourth light, Eleleth. Sophia
brings forth Yaltabaoth without her consort or the Spirit's consent. The
translation does **not** name that consort Theletos in this passage. Other
texts and manuscript versions need to be checked before supplying a name or
presenting this structure as universal.

The same translation gives Yaltabaoth alternative names Saklas and Samael
and attributes to him a boast resembling biblical declarations of divine
uniqueness. This is a provocative rereading of biblical language, not evidence
that all ancient groups called Gnostic interpreted the divine name the same
way. The chat's direct “Monad = light, Barbelo = darkness” pairing also
differs from this text, which describes Barbelo's light as like the Spirit's.
