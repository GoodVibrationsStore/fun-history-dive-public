# Mothers, Divided Heirs, and Sacrificial Sons

## The clue

One chat response proposes a recurring plot: a mother protects a hidden,
disinherited elder while a visible son serves as an unknowing sacrifice.
It compares Jesus and John with Jacob and Esau, Baldr and Hodr, Karna and
Arjuna, and Romulus and Remus. This is a useful **creative pattern to
test**, not a plot that every source actually tells.

| Story and source | What happens in that source | Where the proposed pattern breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Genesis 25 and 27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%2025%3A21-34%2C27%3A1-40&version=NRSVUE) | Rebekah hears an oracle that the elder will serve the younger. Esau sells his birthright; later Rebekah orchestrates the deception by which Jacob receives Isaac's blessing. | Jacob knows he is pretending to be Esau; he is not an unknowing sacrificial son. The birthright sale and the blessing are separate episodes. The oracle complicates the claim that divine order simply favored the firstborn. |
| [Snorri's *Gylfaginning*, chapter 49](https://mythopedia.com/library/prose-edda-brodeur-1916/gylfaginning/) | Frigg secures promises to protect Baldr. Loki exploits mistletoe and directs blind Hodr's throw, killing Baldr. | Frigg is trying to protect Baldr, not arranging his sacrifice to shield Hodr. The chapter does not say Baldr and Hodr are twins. |
| [*Mahabharata*, book 5, sections 145–146](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-mahabharata-book-5-udyoga-parva/section-cxlv) and [146](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-mahabharata-book-5-udyoga-parva/section-cxlvi) | Kunti tells Karna that she is his mother and Surya his father, and asks him to join his brothers. Karna objects to his abandonment, remains loyal to his allies, and promises not to kill her other sons except in his contest with Arjuna. | Karna is the one associated with the sun in this passage. He knowingly chooses his allegiance; the passage does not say Kunti created a decoy sacrifice to protect a secret line. |
| [Luke 1:36–45](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%201%3A36-45&version=NRSVUE) and [Matthew 3:13–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%203%3A13-17&version=NRSVUE) | Luke calls Elizabeth Mary's relative; Matthew portrays John hesitating to baptize Jesus, then consenting. | Neither passage describes a secret transfer of a hereditary title or a pact to make Jesus a decoy. John's words in Matthew do not reveal the private thoughts imagined by the chat. |

These are translations of particular works and episodes, not an exhaustive
survey of each tradition. The chat also compares Romulus and Remus; that
comparison remains a lead to check against specific ancient versions, not a
confirmed match to the whole proposed pattern. Similarity in a mother–son
relationship, a wilderness setting, or a contested inheritance is not by
itself evidence of shared identity, genetic descent, or literary borrowing.

## Two details worth separating

- In [John 19:25–27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2019%3A25-27&version=NRSVUE),
  Jesus entrusts his mother to “the disciple whom he loved.” This passage
  does **not name the disciple** or call him John the Baptist. Other
  traditions identify the beloved disciple differently; they should not be
  silently substituted for this text.
- [Mark 15:34, Matthew 27:46, and Psalm 22:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A1%3BMark%2015%3A34%3BMatthew%2027%3A46&version=NRSVUE)
  share the cry of abandonment. Readers interpret the psalm's relationship
  to the crucifixion in different ways; the words alone do not disclose
  that Jesus learned of a hidden family arrangement.

## Did resurrection undo the sacrifice?

The chat raises a theological question: if Jesus was raised, did his death
still “count”? In [1 Corinthians 15:3–8, 14–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%2015%3A3-8%2C14-20&version=NRSVUE),
Paul reports both Christ's death for sins and his resurrection, and argues
that Christian faith would be futile without the resurrection. That is
evidence of an early Christian theological position, not historical proof
of a resurrection. Christians explain atonement in differing ways; neither
penal substitution nor a single “infinite debt” formula represents every
tradition. A skeptical assessment can question the premise or moral logic
without treating believers' interpretations as identical.
