# Egyptian: Creation, Divine Family, and Renewal

## Starting evidence

[Atum](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Atum) is an Egyptian creator and
solar figure, associated with emergence from Nun, the primordial waters,
and with the evening sun. Egyptian creation accounts varied by place and
period; do not treat one account as the only Egyptian belief.

In the Heliopolitan family tradition, Atum produces Shu and Tefnut; their
descendants include Geb and Nut, and then Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys.
This is a **mythic divine genealogy**, not a documented line of human mothers.
Different texts and forms of the deities deserve separate checking.

[Sekhmet](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sekhmet) is associated with both
destructive force and healing. Those roles are useful for the chat's
question about opposites within one figure, but neither establishes that
Sekhmet is the Rebis, Sophia, or any particular person.

## Comparisons to investigate

- **Waters before creation:** Nun and Mesopotamian Tiamat are different
  figures or concepts in different traditions. Compare specific narratives
  before suggesting a historical connection.
- **Night and solar return:** Atum's setting-sun and renewal imagery is a
  possible symbolic comparison to cycles in the story. It does not document
  repeated destruction and recreation of the universe.
- **Divine family:** Isis and Osiris's story is a separate lead for family,
  protection, and restoration; do not assume their identities or parentage
  carry over into Greek, biblical, or alchemical stories.

## Alexandria and the loss of writings

The chat's songs treat the Library of Alexandria as deliberately burned to
erase hidden history and alternately name Alexander and Caesar. Separate the
chronology: Alexander founded the city in 331 BCE; the library was founded
under the Ptolemies after his death. Ancient accounts connect a fire during
Julius Caesar's Alexandrian campaign in 48 BCE to lost books, but differ on
the extent of the damage. Later events affected the city and the Serapeum.

[Britannica's historical account](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Library-of-Alexandria/The-fate-of-the-Library-of-Alexandria)
and [History's overview](https://www.history.com/articles/library-of-alexandria-destroyed)
differ on the fate of the main collection; the latter emphasizes decline
over time. Neither establishes that the fires targeted a secret genealogy.
Loss of sources is important, but it does not identify what an unknown
scroll said or prove why it was lost.
