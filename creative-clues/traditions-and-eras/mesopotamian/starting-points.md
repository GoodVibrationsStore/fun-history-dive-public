# Mesopotamian: Sky, Descent, and Divine Authority

## Starting evidence

- [An/Anu](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/an/index.html):
  Sumerian An and Akkadian Anu are names for a Mesopotamian sky deity.
  ORACC identifies definite attestations by the mid-third millennium BCE and
  notes that earlier signs can be ambiguous. His standing and family
  relationships change in different traditions.
- [Inana/Ishtar](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/amgg/listofdeities/inanaitar/index.html):
  associated with love, war, and the planet Venus. Her descent into the
  underworld is a lead for the story's descent-and-return motif.
- [*Enuma elish*](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Enuma-Elish): a later
  Babylonian creation narrative presents primeval Tiamat and Apsu, generations
  of gods, and Marduk's rise to kingship. Its story's internal order is not
  the order in which the named gods first appear in historical records.

## Leads for the case

| Question | Current assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Is An the oldest god? | His early attestation is an important clue, not proof he is the first god believed in or the ultimate divine source. |
| Is Inanna the same as Sophia? | Both have been discussed through descent and restoration motifs, but the distinct texts do not establish one identity or a maternal genetic line. |
| Is Tiamat the dark mother in every later story? | She is a primordial figure in a particular Babylonian narrative. Similar water or dragon imagery elsewhere is not enough to establish identity. |
| Did a reset preserve one sacred family? | These sources do not establish that. A comparison with the chat's imagined resets is creative. |

## Gilgamesh and the plant

The chat's historical song also mentions Gilgamesh seeking a flower of
eternal life. In [tablet XI of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/mesopotamian/gilgamesh/tab11.htm),
Utanapishtim tells him about a thorny plant that restores youth. Gilgamesh
retrieves it, intending to take it home and test it, but a snake carries it
away and sheds its skin. The plant's named effect in this translation is
renewed youth; the passage does not say Gilgamesh ate it or became immortal.
This episode is distinct from the flood narrative earlier in the tablet.
